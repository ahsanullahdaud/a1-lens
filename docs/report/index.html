Summary

What it is, and what it found

I wanted to understand A1's catalogue and pricing model from the inside before I joined, so I built a tool that reads the public site the way Google and a shopper do, scores every listing, and compares prices with the high street. Everything below comes from that tool; nothing internal was used.

1 · Crawl1,000 product pages from the public sitemap, one every 2 seconds, robots.txt respected.
→
2 · ParseWhat a shopper sees (price, condition, specs, images) and what Google sees (JSON-LD, meta tags).
→
3 · Audit27 rules, each with a why it matters and a fix. Score 0–100 per listing.
→
4 · Price lensSame product at other retailers, matched by EAN and variant, with the evidence for every price.

Finding 1 · Catalogue

Most of the catalogue is one un-enriched import

565 listings look nothing like the rest of the site. They share a signature: a spec table containing only Brand and EAN, a description under 100 characters and a single image. That is what a distributor feed looks like when only its identifiers are mapped — which means the fix is one import job, not 565 pieces of content work.

Finding 2 · Listing quality

Pricing anomalies the rules surfaced

  • Grades that leapfrog (9): Open Box above Brand New on five Samsung flagships (S25 Ultra 512GB: £779.99 vs £711.99); Refurbished above New on the Galaxy Tab A11 and DJI Osmo Pocket 3; “Good” above “Excellent” on the iPhone 14. A side-effect of pricing each grade by its own winning supplier bid.
  • Search price ≠ landing price (31): the structured data carries the cheapest grade's price while the page opens on a dearer one — a Merchant Center mismatch waiting to happen.
  • Variant not stated: the Galaxy Watch8 and Watch7 never say Bluetooth or LTE in the name or specs; only a “BT” buried in Box contents.

How the comparison stays honest

  • Exact variant or it doesn't count. Colour, capacity and bundle are checked against A1's listing; a near-match (Currys' black Flip 7) is shown but never sets the gap.
  • Main product's own price only. Accessories, care plans, “£14/month” finance and delivery surcharges are set aside and listed as ignored on every row.
  • Evidence on every row. The verbatim snippet the price came from, the retailer's was-price, delivery, stock, source and fetch date.
  • Polite by design. Currys and Argos refuse crawlers, so their pages came through the Parallel Extract API rather than by working around the block.
The exception. The PS5 Pro 2TB sits at £1,009.99 against £789 at both chains and Sony's £699.99 list price — a plain console, not a bundle. Whichever supplier offer is winning that SKU is £220 off the market. The DualSense “was £79” also exceeds any retailer's was-price (list £64.99) and is worth a look under reference-pricing rules.

How it's built · What next

Built to be extended, and to behave

The Overview: segments, findings by rule, score distribution — filterable by segment.

Stack

Next.js 16 (App Router) · TypeScript · Postgres 17 with Drizzle ORM · cheerio · Vitest (59 tests on synthetic fixtures) · Parallel Extract API for retailers that block crawlers · eBay Browse API adapter ready for keys. Every rule carries a why and a fix; adding one is a single object and a test.

Crawling etiquette

  • Public pages only; robots.txt obeyed for every host, including Crawl-delay.
  • One request every 2 seconds per host, honest User-Agent with a contact address.
  • Stops at the first 401/403/429 — Currys and Argos both refused, and were left alone.
  • Parsed fields stored, not page copies; test fixtures are synthetic.

A note on tone. This was built from the outside, on public data, to learn — not to grade anyone's work. The most useful thing it says is that the biggest gaps are structural (one import, a few templates, one pricing rule), which is exactly the kind of thing a small engineering change can move.
